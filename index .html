The Complete Waste Guide

Everything you need to know about sorting, preparing and disposing of every major waste category — the do's, the don'ts, and why it actually matters.

🍌 Organic 🧴 Plastic 📄 Paper 🔋 E-Waste 🍾 Glass 🥫 Metal ☣️ Hazardous 👕 Textile 🧱 Construction 💊 Medical
🍌

Organic Waste

Food scraps, garden trimmings & biodegradable material

Organic waste makes up a large share of household trash in most cities, and it's also the easiest category to keep out of landfill entirely. When food scraps, peels, leftover meals and yard trimmings end up in a landfill, they get buried under other trash and decompose without oxygen — producing methane, a greenhouse gas many times more potent than carbon dioxide. Composted instead, that same material breaks down aerobically and turns into nutrient-rich soil within weeks to a few months.

You don't need a backyard to compost. Countertop bins, community compost drop-offs, and worm bins (vermicomposting) all work well in apartments and small spaces. Municipal "wet waste" collection, where available, is the simplest option — just keep it separate from dry recyclables.

What counts as organic waste

  • Fruit and vegetable peels, cores and scraps
  • Cooked or spoiled food, leftovers
  • Eggshells, tea leaves and coffee grounds
  • Garden trimmings, dry leaves and grass clippings
  • Certified compostable packaging (check the label)

✅ Do

  • Drain excess liquid before disposal
  • Keep it separate from plastic-lined packaging
  • Use a vented bin to reduce odor and pests

🚫 Don't

  • Bag it in regular plastic before composting
  • Include meat/dairy in home compost (attracts pests)
  • Mix with hazardous or chemical waste
🌍 Did you know: composting one ton of food waste avoids roughly 0.9 tons of CO₂-equivalent emissions compared to landfilling it.
🧴

Plastic Waste

Bottles, containers, packaging and films

Not all plastic is created equal, and that's the biggest source of recycling confusion. Look for the small numbered triangle (the resin identification code) stamped on most rigid plastic items — it tells you what polymer it's made from, which determines whether your local facility can actually process it.

Resin codes at a glance

  • 1 – PET/PETE: water & soda bottles. Widely recyclable.
  • 2 – HDPE: milk jugs, shampoo bottles. Widely recyclable.
  • 3 – PVC: pipes, some packaging. Rarely accepted curbside.
  • 4 – LDPE: bread bags, squeeze bottles. Check local rules.
  • 5 – PP: yogurt tubs, bottle caps. Increasingly accepted.
  • 6 – PS: foam cups, disposable cutlery. Rarely recyclable.
  • 7 – Other: mixed or layered plastics. Usually not recyclable.

Soft plastics like bags, wraps and films usually can't go in curbside bins because they jam sorting machinery — many grocery stores offer a dedicated drop-off bin for these instead.

✅ Do

  • Empty and rinse before recycling
  • Remove caps if your local program requires it
  • Flatten bottles to save space (check local guidance first)

🚫 Don't

  • Recycle greasy or food-contaminated containers
  • Bag your recyclables in a plastic bag (often rejected)
  • Assume every numbered plastic is accepted locally
🌍 Did you know: a PET bottle can take over 400 years to break down in a landfill, but recycled PET can become new bottles, clothing fiber, or carpet.
📄

Paper & Cardboard

Newspaper, office paper, boxes and packaging

Paper is one of the most recyclable materials on the planet — most types can be reprocessed 5 to 7 times before the fibers become too short to reuse. The biggest enemy of paper recycling is contamination: grease, food residue, and wet paper all reduce fiber quality or make a batch unsellable to recyclers entirely.

Commonly accepted

  • Newspaper, magazines and office paper
  • Cardboard boxes (flattened) and paperboard
  • Envelopes, junk mail and paper bags

Usually NOT accepted

  • Pizza boxes with grease stains (compost the greasy part instead)
  • Paper cups with a plastic/wax lining
  • Shredded paper (check — some programs want it bagged separately)
  • Tissue, paper towels and napkins (compost instead)

✅ Do

  • Keep paper clean and dry
  • Flatten boxes to save collection space
  • Remove excess tape and plastic windows from envelopes

🚫 Don't

  • Recycle wet or food-soiled paper
  • Include laminated or glossy foil-coated paper
  • Mix with plastic-lined coffee cups
🌍 Did you know: recycling one ton of paper saves about 17 trees and roughly 26,000 liters of water.
🔋

E-Waste

Batteries, phones, computers & electronics

E-waste is the fastest-growing waste stream in the world, and it's also one of the most hazardous when handled incorrectly. Batteries and circuit boards contain heavy metals like lead, mercury and cadmium that can leach into soil and water if crushed in a regular garbage truck or landfill. At the same time, electronics contain valuable recoverable materials — gold, copper, aluminum and rare earth elements — making proper e-waste recycling both a safety issue and a resource-recovery opportunity.

Never put these in household trash

  • Any type of battery — alkaline, lithium-ion, or button cell
  • Phones, tablets and laptops
  • Chargers, cables and small appliances
  • CFL/fluorescent bulbs (contain mercury)

Most manufacturers and many retailers run take-back programs for old devices. Municipal e-waste drop-off events and authorized recyclers are the safest disposal route — never open a battery or attempt to extract components yourself.

✅ Do

  • Wipe or factory-reset devices to remove personal data
  • Tape over battery terminals before disposal/transport
  • Use an authorized e-waste collector or manufacturer take-back

🚫 Don't

  • Throw batteries or electronics in the trash or curbside recycling
  • Puncture, crush or burn batteries
  • Store damaged lithium batteries near flammable material
🌍 Did you know: less than 20% of global e-waste is formally recycled — the rest often ends up in landfills or informal, unsafe recycling operations.
🍾

Glass Waste

Bottles, jars and glass packaging

Glass is 100% and infinitely recyclable without any loss in quality or purity — a glass bottle can be melted down and remade into a new bottle indefinitely. That said, not all glass is the same: container glass (bottles and jars) has a different melting point and composition than window glass, mirrors, or drinking glasses, so mixing them can ruin an entire recycling batch.

Accepted

  • Glass bottles (beverage, sauce, oil)
  • Glass jars (jam, pickles, baby food)

Usually NOT accepted with container glass

  • Window panes, mirrors and light bulbs
  • Drinking glasses, ceramics and Pyrex
  • Broken glass mixed with other trash

✅ Do

  • Rinse out food and liquid residue
  • Remove lids (recycle separately based on material)
  • Wrap broken glass safely before disposal

🚫 Don't

  • Mix drinking glass or ceramics with bottle glass
  • Recycle glass with food still inside
  • Toss broken glass loose in a recycling bin
🌍 Did you know: recycled glass melts at a lower temperature than raw materials, cutting furnace energy use by up to 30%.
🥫

Metal Waste

Cans, foil, and scrap metal items

Metals — especially aluminum — are among the most valuable and energy-efficient materials to recycle. Recycling aluminum uses roughly 95% less energy than producing it from raw ore, which is why aluminum cans are often back on a store shelf as a new can within about 60 days of being recycled.

Commonly accepted

  • Aluminum cans (soda, beer)
  • Steel/tin food cans
  • Clean aluminum foil and trays

Needs special handling

  • Aerosol cans (must be fully empty; check local rules)
  • Scrap metal, appliances and pipes (take to a scrap facility)
  • Paint cans with residue (hazardous waste facility)

✅ Do

  • Rinse food residue from cans and trays
  • Crush cans to save space if your program allows it
  • Ball up small pieces of foil so they don't blow away in sorting

🚫 Don't

  • Include sharp or jagged scrap in curbside bins
  • Recycle cans still containing food
  • Toss pressurized aerosol cans in general trash
🌍 Did you know: an aluminum can recycled today can be back on a store shelf as a new can in as little as 60 days.
☣️

Hazardous Household Waste

Chemicals, paints, pesticides & cleaning products

Household hazardous waste (HHW) covers anything that's flammable, corrosive, toxic, or reactive — a category most people don't think about until they're staring at a half-empty can of paint thinner. These items need dedicated handling because they can contaminate groundwater, react dangerously with other waste in a collection truck, or catch fire if compacted. Unlike your weekly recycling, hazardous waste almost never goes in a curbside bin of any kind.

Most cities run periodic hazardous-waste collection events or maintain a permanent drop-off facility — check your municipal website before assuming something can't be recycled at all. Many components, like used motor oil and old paint, actually can be reprocessed when handled through the right channel.

Common household hazardous items

  • Paints, solvents, thinners and varnishes
  • Pesticides, herbicides and fertilizers
  • Motor oil, antifreeze and automotive fluids
  • Household cleaners, drain openers and bleach
  • Propane tanks and other pressurized canisters

✅ Do

  • Keep items in their original, labeled containers
  • Store away from children and heat sources until drop-off
  • Take to a certified HHW facility or collection event

🚫 Don't

  • Pour chemicals down a drain or into soil
  • Mix different chemicals together for disposal
  • Put pressurized canisters in household trash
🌍 Did you know: a single liter of used motor oil can contaminate up to one million liters of fresh water if dumped improperly.
👕

Textile & Clothing Waste

Clothes, shoes, linens and fabric scraps

Textiles are one of the most overlooked recyclable categories — most people either donate or trash old clothing, without realizing there's a middle path. Even garments too worn or stained to donate can often be recycled into insulation, industrial rags, or fiber for new textiles, provided they're kept dry and out of general trash where they'd otherwise be incinerated or landfilled.

What to do with different conditions

  • Good condition: donate to a charity or thrift store
  • Wearable but unwanted: consignment, swap events, or resale apps
  • Worn out or stained: textile recycling bin (not general trash)
  • Shoes: many brands and stores run take-back programs

✅ Do

  • Keep textiles dry before drop-off
  • Bundle or bag items for the specific collection point
  • Pair up shoes so they don't get separated

🚫 Don't

  • Throw torn or stained clothing in general trash
  • Donate items that are wet, moldy, or contaminated
  • Assume all fabric blends recycle the same way
🌍 Did you know: the equivalent of one garbage truck of textiles is landfilled or burned every second globally.
🧱

Construction & Demolition Waste

Wood, concrete, drywall, tiles and renovation debris

Construction and demolition (C&D) debris is bulky, heavy, and generated in large volumes even from small home projects — a single bathroom renovation can produce hundreds of kilograms of waste. Much of it is inert (concrete, brick, tile) and can be crushed and reused as aggregate for new construction, while wood, metal fixtures and fittings often have separate recycling streams entirely.

Common materials and their paths

  • Concrete, brick & tile: crushed and reused as road base or aggregate
  • Wood & lumber: reused, chipped for mulch, or recycled into particleboard
  • Metal fixtures & pipes: scrap metal recycling
  • Drywall/plasterboard: specialized recycling where available; otherwise landfill
  • Asbestos-containing material: never DIY — requires licensed hazardous removal

✅ Do

  • Separate materials on-site as you demolish, when possible
  • Use a licensed C&D waste hauler for large projects
  • Check for a local construction-debris recycling facility

🚫 Don't

  • Mix suspected asbestos material with regular debris
  • Dump construction waste in general household bins
  • Burn treated or painted wood
🌍 Did you know: construction and demolition debris accounts for roughly a third of all solid waste generated worldwide.
💊

Medical & Pharmaceutical Waste

Expired medicine, sharps, and medical packaging

Medical waste from households — expired medication, used syringes, and related packaging — needs careful handling because it can pose infection risk or contaminate water supplies if flushed or landfilled carelessly. Many pharmacies and clinics operate take-back programs specifically so unused medication doesn't end up in the trash or plumbing system.

Safe disposal by item type

  • Unused/expired medication: pharmacy take-back programs or designated drop boxes
  • Needles & sharps: a rigid, puncture-proof sharps container, then a collection site — never loose in trash
  • Empty pill bottles: remove the label, then recycle as plastic if accepted locally
  • Bandages, gauze: general trash unless visibly contaminated with blood/fluids, which needs biohazard handling

✅ Do

  • Use a pharmacy take-back program whenever available
  • Store sharps in a dedicated puncture-proof container
  • Remove personal information from prescription labels

🚫 Don't

  • Flush medication down the toilet or sink
  • Throw loose needles or sharps in household trash
  • Share or use expired medication
🌍 Did you know: flushed medications have been detected in waterways worldwide, which is why take-back programs are now the recommended standard.

🌱 Eco Learning for Every Age

Simple, safe waste lessons for babies' families, young children, students, adults and older people. The goal is the same: understand waste, stay safe and protect our environment.

Choose Your Learning Level

Adults should supervise children around waste. Children should never handle sharp, medical, chemical, battery or unknown waste.

👶 Newborn & Baby Families

Babies create special household waste, especially diapers, wipes, food containers and packaging. The most important rule is hygiene: keep used diapers and contaminated wipes away from food areas and children's reach.

  • Used diapers: place securely in the general/residual waste stream according to your local municipal rules; they are normally not placed in ordinary recycling.
  • Baby wipes: put used wipes in residual/general waste unless your local authority specifically provides another route. Never flush wipes just because the label says “flushable” unless local sewer guidance explicitly allows it.
  • Baby-food jars: empty and rinse glass jars, then recycle as container glass where accepted.
  • Milk/formula packaging: empty first and check whether the packet is recyclable locally; multilayer packets often need a special collection route.
  • Outgrown clothes: wash and donate if usable; textile recycling is useful for damaged fabric.
  • Toys: repair, donate or reuse before considering disposal. Keep batteries separate from toys.

🧒 Kids: Learn the 5-Step Waste Habit

Kids can learn recycling as a simple game: Stop → Look → Separate → Ask → Reuse.

  • Look at the item before putting it into a bin.
  • Put food scraps into the organic/wet-waste stream where available.
  • Keep clean paper, cardboard, metal and accepted plastic separate.
  • Ask an adult before touching batteries, bulbs, chemicals, medicines, broken glass or unknown waste.
  • Choose reusable bottles, lunch boxes and bags when practical.
  • Turn learning into a challenge: correctly identify 10 household items and earn Eco Points.

🎒 Teens & Students

Students can make a bigger impact by understanding consumption, packaging and e-waste instead of focusing only on the final bin.

  • Use the Smart Waste Sorter before disposing of unfamiliar items.
  • Keep electronics and batteries out of ordinary waste; use authorized collection routes.
  • Reduce disposable cups, bottles and food packaging where practical.
  • Collect clean paper and cardboard separately for recycling.
  • Run school or college waste audits: measure how much organic, recyclable and residual waste is produced.
  • Create posters, QR-code campaigns and classroom recycling points to teach others.

🧑 Adults & Families

Adults usually manage the household waste system. A simple home setup is to keep wet/organic waste separate from dry recyclable materials and special wastes.

  • Separate food and garden waste from dry recyclables.
  • Rinse and dry recyclable containers when local rules require it.
  • Keep batteries, medicines, chemicals and e-waste in separate safe collection streams.
  • Donate or repair usable clothes, furniture, appliances and toys before disposal.
  • For construction debris, paint, pesticides and automotive fluids, use the appropriate authorized collection service.
  • Teach children what they may safely sort and what requires adult handling.

👵 Older Adults

Waste sorting should be easy and safe. Keep frequently used bins clearly labelled and avoid lifting heavy bags or handling sharp materials.

  • Use large, readable labels such as WET, DRY, E-WASTE and SPECIAL WASTE.
  • Ask a family member, caregiver or municipal worker for help with heavy, sharp, chemical or unknown waste.
  • Keep medicines in their original packaging until a safe take-back route is available.
  • Use a stable, easy-to-open recycling container that does not require unsafe bending or lifting.
  • Reuse clean containers and bags when appropriate, but do not reuse containers that held hazardous chemicals for food or drinking water.
🛡️ Universal safety rule: Never let children handle needles, medicines, chemicals, pesticides, batteries, broken glass or unknown waste. Do not burn plastic or mixed household waste. When local recycling instructions differ from this website, follow your local municipal rules.

Everyday Waste, Explained

Local collection rules can differ, so use this as an educational guide and check your municipality for the final disposal route.

BABY

👶 Diapers

Used disposable diapers are generally residual/general waste, not ordinary recycling. Seal them appropriately and keep them away from children and food.

BABY

🧻 Baby Wipes

Used wipes normally belong in residual/general waste. Do not put them in recycling. Avoid flushing wipes unless local sewer guidance specifically permits it.

PACKAGING

🥛 Milk Packets

Empty and drain the packet. Rinse if required by the local program. Many flexible or multilayer packets need a special collection route.

ORGANIC

🍌 Food Scraps

Fruit and vegetable peels, leftovers and suitable garden waste can go to organic/wet-waste collection or composting where available.

PLASTIC

🧴 Plastic Bottles

Empty and rinse. Check the resin code and local acceptance rules before recycling.

PAPER

📦 Cardboard

Flatten clean, dry boxes. Remove excessive plastic, food residue and other non-paper materials.

GLASS

🫙 Glass Jars

Empty and rinse container glass. Keep window glass, mirrors, ceramics and heat-resistant cookware out of ordinary bottle-and-jar glass recycling.

METAL

🥫 Food Cans

Empty and rinse steel or aluminium cans. Sharp scrap metal may require a separate collection route.

E-WASTE

🔋 Batteries

Never put batteries in ordinary household waste or normal recycling unless your local program explicitly accepts them. Use an authorized battery/e-waste collection point.

E-WASTE

📱 Phones & Tablets

Use an authorized e-waste recycler or take-back service. Back up and remove personal data before handing over a working device.

E-WASTE

💡 Bulbs

Fluorescent/CFL bulbs may contain mercury and require a suitable collection route. Handle all bulbs carefully and follow local rules.

TEXTILE

👕 Clothes

Donate wearable clothing. Dry, worn-out textiles may be accepted by textile recyclers. Do not donate wet, mouldy or contaminated items.

SPECIAL

🍳 Used Cooking Oil

Do not pour used cooking oil into drains. Let it cool, store it in a suitable closed container and use a local collection/recycling route where available.

HAZARDOUS

🎨 Paint

Keep paint in its original labelled container and use a household hazardous-waste collection service where available. Never mix chemicals together.

HAZARDOUS

🧪 Cleaning Chemicals

Do not mix cleaners. Keep them away from children and heat, and use the appropriate hazardous-waste route.

MEDICAL

💊 Medicines

Use an authorized pharmacy/medicine take-back route where available. Do not share expired medicines or dispose of them carelessly in drains.

MEDICAL

🩹 Bandages

Household bandages generally belong in residual waste. Follow healthcare-provider or local biohazard guidance when waste is contaminated.

CONSTRUCTION

🧱 Bricks & Concrete

Large quantities should go through a construction-and-demolition waste service. Materials may be crushed and reused.

CONSTRUCTION

🪵 Wood

Reuse good timber. Untreated wood may be chipped or recycled by suitable facilities; painted or treated wood needs the correct local route.

PACKAGING

🥤 Thermocol / Foam

Foam packaging is often not accepted in ordinary recycling. Keep it clean and check for a specialized collection point.

PACKAGING

🧃 Tetra Pak / Cartons

Empty and flatten cartons. Their acceptance depends on local recycling infrastructure, so check the local collection program.

TEXTILE

👟 Shoes

Donate usable shoes or use a take-back program. Pair shoes together before drop-off.

BULKY

🛋️ Furniture

Repair, donate or reuse first. Large damaged furniture may require a municipal bulky-waste or specialized collection service.

BULKY

🧸 Toys

Clean and reuse or donate safe toys. Toys containing batteries or electronics should be separated into the appropriate e-waste stream when discarded.

PAPER

🧻 Tissues & Paper Towels

Used tissues and paper towels are usually not placed in paper recycling. Follow local organic/compost or residual-waste guidance.

SPECIAL

🪴 Garden Waste

Leaves, grass and plant trimmings can often go to organic collection or composting. Keep plastic pots and other materials separate.

E-WASTE

💻 Computers

Use authorized e-waste recycling or take-back. Remove personal data and do not dismantle batteries or electronics yourself.

HAZARDOUS

🛢️ Motor Oil

Never pour used oil onto soil or into drains. Store it safely and use an authorized collection or recycling route.

🌿 Green Technology Explained

The bigger sustainability picture behind EcoSort — how rainwater harvesting, electric vehicles and solar power actually work, in plain language.

🌧️

Rainwater Harvesting System

Rainwater harvesting collects and stores rain instead of letting it run off, so it can be used later or sent back into the ground to recharge groundwater.

  • Rain falls on a catchment surface — usually a rooftop or a paved courtyard.
  • Gutters and downpipes channel the water off the roof and away from the building.
  • A first-flush diverter discards the initial, dirtiest flow so leaves and dust don't enter the system.
  • The remaining water passes through a mesh and sand/gravel filter to remove debris.
  • Filtered water flows into a storage tank for household use, or into a recharge pit/borewell to soak back into the soil.
  • An overflow pipe safely diverts any extra water once the tank is full.
💡 A 100 m² roof can collect roughly 60,000+ litres of water in a year with average rainfall — reducing dependence on groundwater and municipal supply.
🚗⚡

How an Electric Vehicle Works

An EV replaces the fuel tank and engine of a normal car with a battery pack and an electric motor, so it runs on electricity instead of petrol or diesel.

  • The battery pack stores electrical energy, much like a large, rechargeable version of a phone battery.
  • Pressing the accelerator sends a signal to the controller, which regulates how much power flows from the battery.
  • The electric motor converts that electrical energy into mechanical energy, spinning the wheels directly — no gears or engine needed.
  • When you brake or lift off the accelerator, regenerative braking runs the motor in reverse as a generator, sending some energy back into the battery.
  • When the battery runs low, plugging into a charging point refills it, ready for the next trip.
💡 EVs have no tailpipe emissions and far fewer moving parts than petrol/diesel engines, which means lower running and maintenance costs.
🌳🤝

Shramdaan–Hariyali Van (श्रमदान–हरियाली वन)

This term blends two Hindi ideas into one: community-powered greening. It usually describes an afforestation drive, community plantation, or public garden built and cared for by local citizens, students or volunteers — rather than left entirely to municipal contractors.

  • Shramdaan (श्रमदान): from shram (physical effort/labour) + daan (donation/giving) — voluntary, unpaid labour offered for a community or public benefit.
  • Hariyali (हरियाली): greenery, lush vegetation, environmental freshness.
  • Van (वन): forest, woodland, or grove.
  • Together: a Shramdaan–Hariyali Van is a forest or green patch raised through people's own labour rather than paid contract work.
  • Volunteers typically dig pits, plant saplings, build tree guards, and take turns watering and weeding until the young trees are established.
  • Because the community plants and maintains it, survival rates are often higher than contractor-driven plantations, and the space doubles as a shared resource — shade, fuel-wood, fodder, or a small park.
💡 EcoSort connection: a Shramdaan–Hariyali Van is a great next step after home composting — the compost and mulch your household produces can directly nourish a community-planted grove like this.

How Solar Energy Works

Solar panels can power exactly the kind of low-energy smart devices EcoSort relies on — bin-fill sensors, moisture sensors and lid motors — using sunlight instead of the mains supply.

  • Each solar panel is made of many photovoltaic (PV) cells, typically silicon semiconductors.
  • Sunlight striking a cell knocks electrons loose, creating a flow of electric current — this is called the photovoltaic effect.
  • The panel produces direct current (DC) electricity, which a charge controller regulates to protect the battery from overcharging.
  • A battery stores surplus energy generated during the day so devices keep running at night or on cloudy days.
  • An inverter converts stored DC power into alternating current (AC) if the connected devices need AC; low-power sensors and motors can often run directly on DC.
  • The result is a self-sufficient power supply for smart bins, sensors and lid actuators, with no cabling to the grid required.
💡 Pairing a small solar panel with a battery lets a Smart Dustbin sensor, the Wet/Dry sensor and the automatic lid all run off-grid, anywhere they're placed.

About EcoSort

We're on a mission to make recycling less confusing and more rewarding, one sorted item at a time.

Our Story

EcoSort started as a weekend project at the Clean & Green Hackathon, built by a small team frustrated by how often recyclable items ended up in landfill simply because people weren't sure which bin they belonged in.

What began as a simple waste-sorting lookup has grown into a small community platform — combining a smart sorter, practical tips, and an Eco Points system that turns everyday recycling habits into something worth celebrating.

We're still early, still learning, and still adding features based on what our community tells us is actually useful. Thanks for being part of it.
